1 Introduction
This project proposal outlines the planned development of a procedurally generated dungeon-based game called Silent Labyrinth by a small student team. The project focuses on designing a modular game system that will incorporate procedural world generation, puzzle-driven gameplay mechanics, and dynamic environmental elements. The team will be responsible for planning the full development lifecycle, including project management, system design, implementation planning, testing strategies, and quality assurance processes, before developing the game according to the proposed design.


The project addresses the challenge of creating replayable and engaging non-violent dungeon exploration gameplay within the constraints of a student project, where both development time and available resources are limited. While the dungeon-crawler genre has experienced significant growth and remains popular among players, mainstream titles are predominantly focused on combat-oriented gameplay. This creates an opportunity to explore an alternative approach centred on puzzle-solving, exploration, and environmental interaction. Traditional level design can also result in repetitive and predictable experiences; therefore, procedural generation has been selected to increase replayability while maintaining structured gameplay through controlled dungeon layouts and modular puzzle systems.


The proposed game will combine an overworld generated through procedural techniques with interconnected dungeon environments containing a variety of puzzle-based challenges. Core gameplay will focus on exploration, progression, and puzzle-solving rather than combat, with systems designed to support player progression, world persistence, and replayability. The expected outcome of this project is a complete design and implementation plan for a playable prototype that demonstrates the feasibility of these concepts.


This report provides an overview of the project's background and supporting literature, including the identified market gap, relevant development technologies, and project management methodologies. It then presents the proposed project management plan, covering project scope, team organisation, scheduling, resource requirements, and risk management strategies. The proposed methodology section details the planned development tools, development stages, and testing approach that will be used throughout the project. Finally, the report concludes with a summary of the proposal and expected outcomes, with supporting references and appendices included for completeness.

2 Background and Literature Review
2.1 Background Introduction
The dungeon-crawler genre has experienced significant market growth, yet no mainstream title offers a non-violent, puzzle-driven experience. This review examines four key areas relevant to this project: university student preferences and the case for non-violent game design, the roguelike and dungeon-crawler market, 2D development frameworks, and project management methodologies. Together, these areas establish context and reveal the gap this project is positioned to fill.
2.2 Literature Review
2.2.1 Target Audience and Market Gap
PC gaming is broadly accessible to the target demographic: Bhalla [1] reports 96% of university students own a laptop or desktop, confirming a PC-based game can reach this audience without platform exclusivity concerns. Millamena et al. [2] surveyed 100 undergraduates and found that adventure and strategy games in fantasy contexts are the most preferred genres, directly supporting the genre chosen for this project.

The broader market further validates the dungeon-crawler format's commercial appeal. Pednekar [3] reports the roguelike market was valued at $3.2 billion USD in 2024, projected to grow at 10.5% annually. However, the dominant titles: The Binding of Isaac [4], Hades [5], and Enter the Gungeon [6] all rely on combat as their primary mechanic. No mainstream dungeon-crawler offers a non-violent, puzzle-driven alternative, leaving a clear and unaddressed gap in this growing market.


The closest competing titles are The Legend of Zelda series [7] and Portal 2 [8]. Zelda demonstrates that puzzle-solving and dungeon exploration can serve as the structural foundation of a commercially successful game; however, combat remains integral to its loop, disqualifying it as a non-violent alternative. Portal 2 removes combat entirely in favour of pure puzzle mechanics but lacks the stealth, inventory, and enemy-avoidance mechanisms fundamental to this project. Neither title provides the precise combination sought, confirming the gap remains unfulfilled.

Although causation remains contested in the literature, Anderson et al. [9] conducted a meta-analytic review that found consistent associations between violent video game exposure and increased aggressive cognition and behaviour. This provides academic grounds for developing a non-violent alternative that retains cognitive engagement. Ryan, Rigby, and Przybylski [10] applied Self-Determination Theory to video games, demonstrating that intrinsic player motivation is driven by competence, autonomy, and relatedness, constructs that do not require combat to fulfil. Thus supporting the case that a puzzle-driven approach can sustain player engagement as effectively as combat-based mechanics. Together, commercial demand, psychological evidence, and motivational theory form three independent justifications for this project's non-violent, puzzle-driven approach.
2.2.2 Development Tools and Frameworks
Two 2D frameworks were evaluated: Unity [11] and MonoGame [12]. Unity offers extensive 3D and cross-platform capabilities, but these exceed the needs of a 2D puzzle-adventure game and introduce unnecessary performance overhead. MonoGame, a lightweight C# framework, provides direct control over rendering and UI implementation, enabling the minimalist interface design advocated by Schell [13], without Unity's editorial complexity. MonoGame therefore offers the most appropriate balance of control, efficiency, and simplicity for this project.
2.2.3 Project Management Methodologies
Scrum is the dominant agile methodology in game development. Keith [14] argues its practices are well-suited to the experimental and rapidly evolving nature of game projects, contrasting with waterfall approaches ill-suited to frequent design pivots and emerging requirements. The continuous playtesting embedded in each sprint also functions as ongoing user testing, ensuring player-centred feedback throughout development. Jondya et al. [15] applied a Scrum methodology to a student-developed puzzle adventure game of comparable scope, reporting positive results in team coordination, incremental feature delivery, and overall product quality. Their case study provides a validated precedent for applying Scrum to a student-led puzzle adventure game, and thus is the management framework adopted for this project. 
2.3 Conclusion
This literature review creates a unified, multi-directional case for the project. Commercial evidence reveals a large, growing market with an unfilled gap aligned with the target demographic's preferences. Psychological research validates the non-violent, puzzle-driven approach, while competing titles confirm commercial viability and highlight the absence of the specific combination this project targets. MonoGame and Scrum emerge as the most appropriate development framework and methodology for the project's scope and iterative model. Together, the literature provides well-supported justification for the project's rationale, design, and technical decisions.

3 Project Management Plan
3.1 Scope and Requirements
Silent Labyrinth is a 2D single-player puzzle adventure game designed for university students. Players navigate dungeon environments from a bird's-eye perspective, solve puzzles, locate progression items, and evade enemies using stealth rather than combat.


The game features a procedurally generated overworld composed of multiple biomes containing dungeons. This enables players to select their next adventure while ensuring each playthrough presents unique environmental challenges and gameplay experiences.
3.1.1 In Scope
High Priority
* Construction of a game loop with state and event management.
* Creation of unique dungeons with engaging designs and puzzles.
* Creation of game assets.
* Development of a procedural world generation system.
* Development of player movement and interaction systems.
* Design and implementation of a puzzle framework.
* Design and implementation of a 2D sprite rendering system.
* Development of an inventory system for item collection, storage, and usage.
* Addition of NPCs to enhance gameplay and interaction.


Medium Priority
* Implementation of a lighting system to create a dynamic environment.


Low Priority
* Integration of environmental audio.
3.1.2 Out of Scope
* Procedural generation of dungeons and puzzles.
* Particle effects system.
* 3D rendering framework.
* Multiplayer functionality.
3.1.3 Requirement Traceability Matrix
The functional and non-functional requirements for Silent Labyrinth are documented in the Requirement Traceability Matrix (RTM) provided in Appendix C, which has been formally signed off and includes product acceptance criteria. Functional requirements define core gameplay features such as puzzle mechanics, dungeon progression, procedural world generation, and player interactions. Non-functional requirements specify quality attributes including performance, usability, reliability, compatibility, and maintainability.


Requirements were obtained through three primary elicitation methods. First, the development team used introspection to identify features essential to the game's vision, including puzzle-solving mechanics, dungeon progression, and a procedurally generated overworld. Second, existing puzzle adventure games were analysed to identify successful implementations of puzzle systems, progression mechanics, and procedural generation techniques. Finally, informal discussions with university students provided feedback regarding gameplay experience, responsiveness, ease of control, compatibility, and overall game quality. This feedback was primarily used to refine the project's non-functional requirements.
3.2 Project Organisation
Our team has elected to employ the Scrum methodology for software development.
3.2.1 Scrum Overview
Scrum is an Agile software development framework that emphasises iterative and cyclical progress, illustrated below in figure 1. Work is organised into short development cycles known as sprints. The framework is defined by dedicated team roles, structured ceremonies, and a prioritised product backlog that guides development.


  

Figure 1. Typical Scrum Diagram [16]
3.2.1.1 Scrum Team
A typical Scrum team is composed of a Scrum Master, a Product Owner, and Developers.
The Scrum Master is responsible for facilitating the agile process, removing blockers, supporting collaboration, and ensuring the project continuously progresses while following Scrum practices.
The Product Owner is responsible for defining the product vision and managing the product backlog, including defining requirements, prioritising features, and representing stakeholder interests.
The Developers are responsible for building and developing the product. This role is shared by all team members and involves designing, implementing, and testing features, fixing bugs, and fulfilling project requirements.
3.2.1.2 Scrum Backlog
Scrum methodology breaks down features and tasks into user stories, which capture a specific need or goal from the perspective of a user persona. Each user story focuses on a single, atomic piece of functionality and is organised into backlogs.
The Product Backlog contains all user stories and represents everything that makes up the project.
The Sprint Backlog contains the user stories planned for a given sprint and is where developers are assigned specific features for implementation.
The Done Backlog is initially empty and is filled with completed user stories as sprints progress, acting as an archive and record of completion.
3.2.1.3 Scrum Ceremonies
During a Scrum-managed project, there are four ceremonies that make up a sprint cycle.
First, during Sprint Planning, the Product Owner and team prioritise tasks and fill the Sprint Backlog with user stories to be implemented during the sprint, with each story allocated to a developer.
Then, during Implementation, developers implement and test assigned features. The team also typically participates in a daily stand-up meeting where progress is discussed, blockers are identified, and solutions are put in place.
Next, after development is complete, a Sprint Review takes place. The team demonstrates the product to the client, identifies completed features, and discusses feedback to update the backlog.
Finally, at the end of the sprint, a Sprint Retrospective takes place. The Scrum Master leads the discussion as the team reflects on what went well, what challenges were encountered, and what improvements can be made for the next sprint.
3.2.2 Rationale for Choosing Scrum
Scrum was chosen due to its flexibility and iterative nature. Since we are working on a computer game, which is inherently a subjective experience, it is important that we are not only receiving constant feedback from our target users, but also able to change requirements and adjust to deliver a good product by the end of the project.


Additionally, this framework allows our team to collaborate effectively, especially given our relatively small team size, and enables work to be organised into manageable increments. This allows us to easily track project progress and it facilitates early blocker identification which helps ensure we manage to successfully deliver by the end.
3.2.3 Adapting Scrum for Our Project
3.2.3.1 Overview
For our project, we will run 2-week sprints and hold weekly stand-up meetings instead of daily ones.
2-week sprints were chosen to suit the ~10-week project timeline, allowing enough time to make meaningful progress while maintaining an iterative development cycle. Longer sprints would reduce feedback opportunities, while shorter sprints would be too restrictive given student schedules.
Weekly stand-ups were chosen due to competing academic, personal, and professional commitments. While daily stand-ups are common in industry, they are impractical for this project, while weekly check-ins still maintain communication and progress tracking.
3.2.3.2 Additional Roles and Responsibilities
Our team will include three additional specialty roles alongside the core Scrum roles.
The System Architect is responsible for designing and managing the technical structure, guiding technology decisions, establishing architectural standards, and acting as the game engine expert and support point for developers.
The Quality Assurance Lead is responsible for quality testing and control, defining quality standards, metrics, and test plans, and enforcing quality-related policies to ensure a maintainable, high-quality product.
The Art and Design Lead is responsible for designing and producing game artwork, establishing artistic standards, creating design assets, and ensuring a consistent visual style throughout the game.

Remaining project activities include implementing core gameplay systems, developing puzzle mechanics, creating the procedural world generation system, designing assets and user interfaces, conducting testing, resolving defects, and preparing final documentation and presentations. 3.4 Management Process
3.4.1 Risk Management
Effective management of risks, progress and quality is essential to ensuring Silent Labyrinth is delivered within scope, on schedule, and to an acceptable standard. Risk management for this project follows a continuous iterative cycle consisting of identification, analysis, response planning, and monitoring. The team will maintain a dedicated Risk Register (Appendix D) that documents each risk’s description, root cause, triggers, probability of occurrence, impact, owner, response strategies, and the current status of the risk. This register will be monitored and updated regularly during weekly meetings.


Risks are identified through team discussions, progress reviews, playtest feedback, and stakeholder consultations. This ensures that both technical and organisational risks are captured early. Examples include schedule delays (R1), logic bugs preventing level progression (R2), uneven team contribution (R3), and performance issues (R4). 


Each risk is assessed using a probability-impact matrix (Appendix D1), where probabilities/likelihood range from 1 (Rare) to 5 (Almost Certain), and consequences/impact range from 1 (Insignificant) to 5 (Catastrophic). Overall risks are classified as Low, Medium, High or Extreme.


For each risk, the team defines both a risk response (an action to prevent the risk from occurring) and an incident response (a reactive action to take if the risk occurs). This structured approach ensures that risks are not only documented, but actively managed throughout development.


The Risk owner for each risk is responsible for tracking their assigned risk and reporting changes during weekly meetings. They also update the risk status, which is a dynamic attribute that can be Monitored, Triggered or Resolved. This can shift multiple times during development as conditions change and issues emerge
3.4.2 Monitoring and Controlling Mechanisms
To ensure the project remains aligned with planned milestones, the team employs several complementary monitoring and control mechanisms.


All meeting minutes, design decisions, and planning documents are maintained in Google Drive, ensuring a centralised and accessible record of project activity. This integrates well with Monash University’s native Google Suite integration, and allows us to share our work with supervisors easily.


Regular communication will occur through Messenger, Discord and Zoom, enabling the team to easily clarify tasks, escalate issues, and coordinate the development activities and progress. These applications have been chosen for their simplicity and ease of access. They also support early detection of risks such as R3 (uneven contribution) and R10 (technical skill limitation).


For monitoring tasks, the team will use Trello, a Kanban-style project management tool to track tasks across stages such as To Do, In Progress, Testing and Completed. Each task in the Kanban board will also be linked to:
* An activity in the WBS
* One or more team members assigned to the task
* A due date
* Dependencies (where applicable)
Weekly reviews will ensure that tasks align with the Gantt chart and that any slippage is identified early.


For code version control, the team will use Github, which will enable
* Branch-based development
* Pull requests and code reviews
* Merge conflict detection
* Historical tracking of changes
This also reduces the likelihood of R5 (loss of project files or code conflicts), and supports auditability of development progress.

4 Proposed Methodology
4.1 Required Software, Utilities, and Tools
The project will be developed using the MonoGame framework, which provides the core game loop, rendering, input handling, audio, content loading, and other hardware abstractions. MonoGame was chosen for its lightweight simplicity, avoiding the complexity of Unity while removing the need for a custom engine. It is well suited for custom puzzle mechanics, procedural world generation, dynamic lighting, and save management.


All gameplay systems will be implemented in C# on the .NET 10 runtime due to its strong compatibility with MonoGame, smooth transition from Java-based object-oriented design, extensive standard library support, and suitability for modular game development. It will be used for world generation, puzzle logic, collision detection, player interactions, lighting, and save management.


* Development Environment: Visual Studio Code as the primary IDE.
* Version Control: Git with GitHub for repository hosting and collaboration.
* Asset Creation: Aseprite for pixel-art assets (sprites, environments, UI, animations).
* Operating System: Windows 11 as the primary development and testing platform.
4.2 Stages of Development
We plan to develop our project in accordance with this design diagram:


  

Figure 2. Procedurally Generated Game Design Diagram


Core systems will be developed first, including the game loop, input handling, asset loading, save management, and game state management. This establishes the foundation for all later features.


Next, a world generation system will create procedural maps using a seeded process with Perlin Noise and Fractal Brownian Motion (fBm) to generate terrain heightmaps, which are then used to assign biomes and place dungeons. Generated data is exported and loaded at runtime for consistency.


A world map system will then load generated data, manage dungeon selection, and track player progression through the save system.


The dungeon system will handle playable levels, including room-based layouts, procedural or pre-authored room generation, door and transition logic, collision boundaries, and environmental object placement, providing a structured and navigable gameplay space.


Puzzle mechanics will then be integrated as modular systems, focusing on lockpicking puzzles (pin tumbler, multi-stage vault, and damaged locks) and light-based puzzles (limited candle exploration, mirror mazes, and reflective shields).


Rendering and lighting will follow, including sprite rendering, UI elements, particle effects, and a dynamic lighting system where light propagation, occlusion, and reflection directly influence gameplay puzzles.


Finally, all systems will be integrated and tested together to ensure correct data flow, stability, and progression.
4.3 Testing and QA
Testing will be performed throughout development rather than at the end.


Individual systems will be tested as they are developed, with integration testing focusing on level loading, progression tracking, puzzle completion, save reliability, and overall stability.


Playtesting will also be used to identify issues with puzzle difficulty, level design, and player progression, with feedback used to fix bugs and improve gameplay before final submission.
